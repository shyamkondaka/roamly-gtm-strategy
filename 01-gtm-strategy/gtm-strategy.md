# GTM Strategy · Roamly Groups

## 1. Discover Framework

### Align on the Goal
- **Core business problem:** Roamly’s best customers are trying to bring more people along, and the product forces them into workarounds: separate accounts, manual payment chasing, and group chats for availability. Roamly is losing high-intent demand (and the revenue, data, and referrals that come with it) to friction. Groups also turns a single-traveler product into a multi-traveler one. That is a new, larger unit of purchase and a new acquisition channel.
- **What success looks like:** Product: Roamly becomes the system of record for coordinated trips (shared itinerary, payments, roster), not just a booking marketplace. That is the base for later offerings like multi-day trips and corporate programs.
Brand: “Roamly” becomes the default answer to “who’s planning this?” without losing the curated, authentic positioning. Groups must not feel like a tour-bus product.
Customer relationship: Each group converts one organizer into several new accounts. Roamly gets a relationship with every attendee, not just the payer.
Host network: Hosts earn more per session and fill capacity more predictably, and quality scores hold.
Guardrail: Core individual and small-party booking, conversion, and ratings stay flat or improve.

### Do the Research
- **Signals of a real opportunity:** Behavioral: multiple accounts booking the same experience and date in one session or within hours, repeated “check availability” searches for the same slot, abandoned carts at party size 5+, and checkout drop-off at the point of paying for others.
Support: ticket volume and themes (split payment, “add my friend,” date coordination) and manual workarounds agents perform.
Search and discovery: queries with group language (“for 10 people,” “bachelorette,” “team”), and group-size filter usage.
Host side: private-booking requests, hosts declining or manually handling large parties, and capacity that sits unused.
Referral graph: clusters of accounts created close together that book the same experience, which shows informal groups already exist.
Willingness to pay: demand that holds despite the friction. A strong signal is people paying more or doing more work to book for groups anyway.
- **Top 5 possible segments:** Celebration and milestone organizers: bachelor/bachelorette parties, milestone birthdays, friend reunions (6–20 people).
Family trips and reunions: multigenerational groups with mixed ages, budgets, and mobility needs.
Corporate teams and offsites: team-building days and incentive trips, with an employer paying or reimbursing.
Wedding and event guests: destination-wedding parties wanting a shared welcome-day or pre-event activity.
Affinity groups and clubs: alumni, travel clubs, hobby groups, and school or youth trips.
- **How to size the opportunity:** Internal: share of bookings with party size above N, multi-account same-slot clustering, abandoned group carts, GMV and average order value by party size, and support ticket counts. This is the bottom-up core of the sizing.
Customer: a survey and 15–20 interviews with recent organizers on trip frequency, group size, budget, and what they use today.
Host: capacity and appetite by city and experience type, and the maximum group size at which quality holds.
External: group travel market reports, bachelor/bachelorette and reunion trip volume, and competitor group offerings (Airbnb Experiences, GetYourGuide, Viator, Tripadvisor, local DMCs, and WhatsApp-plus-Venmo).
Method: estimate serviceable demand as the number of organizers in our 40 cities × trip frequency × share booking an experience × average group spend. Then separate the demand already in our data (floor) from incremental demand (upside).

### Form Your Hypothesis
- **Most valuable segment to target first, and why:** Segment to target first: celebration and milestone organizers, meaning friends and extended-family groups of roughly 6–20 people planning a single trip moment.

Why this segment first:

Demand is visible and acute. This is the segment most likely behind the multi-account, hand-split workarounds in the data. One organizer carries the whole burden and has the strongest motivation to switch.
Highest-value per organizer, with built-in growth. Large parties mean high booking value. Each group brings 5–15 invitees, many of them new to Roamly, so every launched group is also an acquisition event. Organizers also repeat (the next birthday, the next reunion).
Product fit. Split payments, shared itineraries, and group booking cover their core needs. They don’t need invoicing, procurement approval, or admin controls, which corporate buyers do. That makes this segment ready now rather than after another build cycle.
Self-serve motion. It fits Roamly’s existing consumer funnel, so it launches without a sales team and without risking the core product.
Brand fit. Celebration groups want exactly what Roamly is known for: something authentic and memorable, not a generic tour.
Contained risk. A single-event booking is simpler to operate than multi-day, multi-vendor corporate programs, and hosts can start with capped group sizes.
- **Core value proposition:** With Roamly Groups, the person planning the celebration can book one local experience for the whole party, split the cost automatically, and keep everyone’s plans in one place, without chasing friends for money or juggling separate accounts.

## 2. GTM motion

- **Chosen motion:** _____
- **Why:** Yes, it’s a hybrid. The primary layer is PLG and the secondary layer is CLG. SLG is deferred, not blended in.

The reasoning

The five inputs describe how the segment buys, and every one of them points to a self-serve, product-driven path. The reasoning matters more than the tally, so here is the logic behind each call.

Why PLG is primary: the product carries the entire buying journey.

One person decides, in one session. The organizer finds an experience, sets a party size, and sends an invite link, and nothing in that sequence needs a human. When time to value is minutes and the decision is made alone, adding a salesperson adds delay and cost.
The transaction is large, but the commitment is small. A group booking is a high-value order, not a contract. Organizers buy once to three times a year with no procurement or approval chain. That is the profile of a self-serve product, not a negotiated deal.
Growth is built into the core action. Creating a group sends 5 to 15 invites, and each invitee enters a Roamly flow. The act of using the product is the acquisition channel. SLG cannot do this, because growth there scales with headcount.
It protects what already works. Launching inside the existing consumer funnel means no new motion competes with the core business.

Why CLG is secondary: it amplifies PLG but cannot carry the launch alone.

Celebration planning is peer-influenced. Organizers look to friends, reviews, and travel communities when choosing what to book, and Roamly’s reputation was built in exactly those places. That is a real tailwind.
But CLG is slow and hard to control. Community effects compound over months, and the launch needs to convert existing demand now. Community cannot guarantee a payment gets completed or an invite gets accepted. The product has to do that.
So CLG works best as a multiplier on PLG’s outputs: shareable itineraries, organizer templates, host and creator referrals, and group reviews that make the next organizer’s decision easier.

Why SLG is out: it fails on every input. The buyer is a consumer, ACV is low to mid, the cycle is days, and nothing requires human negotiation. Sales headcount would raise cost without raising conversion.

Primary vs. secondary layers
	Primary: PLG	Secondary: CLG
Role	Acquires and converts organizers and invitees	Builds trust and demand around the product
Mechanism	Group creation, invite links, split payments, shared itinerary	Shareable itineraries, templates, host/creator referrals, group reviews

## 4. The assumption I'm pushing back on

The CMO’s claim that the viral loop is too slow and too passive to power Acquire: “invitees are obligated participants, and organizers book only 1 to 3 times a year, so the loop is a slow referral program.”

Why I’m pushing back

1. The cycle-time math treats the loop as one organizer repeating, but it’s many people.
The CMO divides by one organizer’s booking frequency (6 to 12 months). The loop actually runs across the whole invitee cohort. A group of 10 puts ten people into a Roamly flow at once, and any of them can become the next organizer on their own timeline. Celebration occasions also cluster in the same social circles (bachelorette to wedding to baby shower to milestone birthday), so one group seeds several future occasions at once. The relevant number is invitee-to-organizer conversion within 12 months, multiplied by group size, not organizer repeat rate.

2. “Passive” understates the invitee’s position.
An invitee isn’t a cold lead, but they’re also not a captive one. They’ve just had a good, local, hosted experience with people they care about, which is the strongest possible trial. Roamly’s repeat rate is three times the industry average because the experience itself creates the next purchase. That is the same mechanism, applied to ten people at once.

3. Where I concede.
The CMO is right that “account created” is the wrong event. A friend who pays and never returns isn’t a customer. I’m accepting that part and tightening the definition.

4. Where I’m honest about the limits.
I can’t prove the coefficient from the brief. The CMO and I are both reasoning without a number. The pushback is that the loop is plausible and testable, not that it’s proven.

How this shapes the refined strategy
Keep Acquire as the goal, but redefine the event: an activated invitee is someone who completes a payment and saves an experience, returns for a second session, or starts their own group within 90 days. Account creation is no longer the metric.
Measure the loop by cohort, not by organizer frequency: track invitee-to-organizer conversion at 30, 90, and 365 days, and exclude invitees who already had a Roamly account.
Pre-commit to a kill threshold: if activated-invitee rate or 12-month invitee-to-organizer conversion falls below a floor set before launch, Groups is repositioned as an AOV and conversion feature rather than an acquisition channel.
Design the invitee experience on purpose: the invitee’s first moments (payment, itinerary, post-experience prompt) are where the loop is won or lost, so they get their own product scope instead of being a byproduct of the organizer flow.
Launch narrower: one or two cities and one celebration type (bachelor/bachelorette weekends), where clustering is tightest and the loop can be read fastest.

I’m accepting the CMO’s points on payment-gap policy and pricing as launch requirements, but I’m not accepting that the viral thesis is weak. It’s untested, and the first launch is designed to test it.

## 5. V1 GTM strategy one-pager

Roamly Groups: V1 GTM Strategy One-Pager (Draft)

Goal: Acquire net-new customers by turning each group organizer into a distribution channel.
Launch segment: Bachelor/bachelorette weekend organizers (groups of 6-20) in 1-2 cities.
Value prop: With Roamly Groups, the person planning the celebration can book one local experience for the whole party, split the cost automatically, and keep everyone’s plans in one place, without chasing friends for money or juggling separate accounts.

Motion: PLG-led, with a light CLG layer. PLG proves the loop first, and CLG scales what works. SLG is deferred to a later corporate phase.

Channel approach
Seed from existing demand (weeks 0-4). In-app and email prompts to users already running workarounds (multi-account same-slot bookings, abandoned carts at party size 5+). Near-zero cost and the fastest read on conversion and payment behavior.
In-product loop (primary).
Organizer flow: pick an experience, set party size, send an invite link.
Invitee flow (own product scope): an invite page showing the experience and host, a one-tap share payment, a shared itinerary, and a post-experience prompt to save or plan their own trip.
Shareable itinerary pages double as social proof.
Light CLG (city-level only). Host-led referrals and group sessions surfaced in reviews. No creator programs or paid community seeding until the loop is proven.
Assisted support (safety net, not a channel). Human help triggers on failed payments or parties over 20.
Success metric

North star: activated net-new invitees per group within 90 days.
Activated invitee = no prior Roamly account, completes a payment, and within 90 days saves an experience, books a second session, or starts their own group.
