# Competitive Intelligence & Strategic Bet · Roamly Groups

## 1. Primary competitor

- **Competitor:** The unbundled coordination stack: Partiful for invites and RSVPs, Splitwise and Venmo for splitting and settling money. These aren’t one company. They’re a workflow that organizers assemble themselves, usually on top of the group chat.
- **Core offering:** Partiful: event pages, invites, RSVPs, and group updates.
Splitwise: tracking who owes what and settling up after the fact.
Venmo / Zelle: peer-to-peer payment between friends.
- **How they position themselves:** Partiful: the fun, low-effort way to get friends to show up.
Splitwise: no more awkward money conversations.
Venmo: paying friends back, instantly and socially.
- **Primary audience:** Friend groups in their 20s and 30s, which overlaps heavily with bachelor/bachelorette and milestone organizers. The overlap is the point: your organizer already has these apps installed and uses them habitually.
- **Known pricing or packaging:** Free or near-free for the core use case. Partiful is free to use (verify any premium tier). Splitwise has a free tier with paid upgrades (verify current limits). Venmo and Zelle are free for standard peer transfers, with fees for instant transfers or credit card funding (verify). The takeaway: your organizer’s current cost of coordination is roughly zero in dollars, so Roamly can’t win by charging for features that these tools give away.
- **Why they're the biggest threat:** They own the moment of decision. The group agrees on a plan in the chat or on a Partiful page before anyone opens Roamly. If the plan is already set, Roamly competes for the booking, not the idea.
Zero adoption cost. No new account, no new habit. Your invitees would have to create Roamly accounts, which the stack never asks of them.
They neutralize your core pitch. “Split the cost, keep plans in one place” sounds like a feature those tools already offer, so you risk being seen as a more expensive way to do something free.
They’re a weak substitute, which is your opening. They move money after the fact, so the organizer still fronts the cash and carries the risk. Nothing ties the plan to a confirmed host, a headcount, or a deposit deadline.
They could extend. A Partiful booking integration, or a Venmo-style group-payment feature, would close part of your gap from the adjacent side.

Implication for your strategy
The fight is about workflow, not features. Your differentiator isn’t splitting payments, since they do that. It’s that payment and booking are the same action, and the organizer never fronts the money. That sharpens the value prop to: book it for everyone without paying for everyone. The payment-gap policy is therefore your core product promise, not a back-office detail. A Partiful integration or invite-link import is worth exploring as a way to turn this competitor into a distribution channel.

2	"What happens if three people don't pay?"	Most dangerous. Needs a settled policy before launch (who absorbs the gap, deposit rules, deadlines). If undecided, don't claim to solve it.

## 3. What I want to revisit in my Module 1 strategy

I want to revisit the value proposition (and the “ready now” claim behind it) because the battlecard shows that “split the cost automatically” is the part of our pitch competitors already give away.

## 4. Strategic bet

### Data
- **Who Roamly Groups is for:** Bachelor/bachelorette and milestone-celebration organizers who book for 8 to 20 people and would otherwise put the whole cost on their own card.

This is a narrower cut of the Module 1 segment. It comes from the battlecard’s “Why They Win” list: small or low-stakes groups (under about 6 people, or cheap activities) are fine with Venmo, so the product has no edge there. The pain our edge removes grows with group size and ticket price, because that is where fronting the money and chasing late payers hurt.

The buyer is one person who carries the financial and social risk for everyone else.
- **The one thing worth choosing it for:** The organizer books for everyone without paying for everyone.

Payment and booking are a single action. Each person pays their own share, and the reservation is confirmed once the group is in. Splitting and a shared itinerary are not the reason to choose us, because Venmo, Splitwise, Partiful, and the group chat already offer both free. Moving the financial risk off the organizer is the one thing the free stack cannot do, because it has no inventory, no host relationships, and no way to tie payment to a confirmed booking.
- **The biggest threat to that position:** Roamly failing to actually deliver “you don’t front the money.”

The edge only exists if the unpaid-share policy works. If three of ten friends don’t pay and the organizer still covers the gap, we’ve become a more expensive way to do what Venmo does for free, and the position collapses. That is the weakest point in the battlecard, and it is a decision about policy, risk, and finance that hasn’t been made.

The main external threat sits behind it: a Partiful booking feature or a payments-app group feature would narrow the gap from the other side. That is slower and less certain, so I rank it second.

### Insight
The stack wins before anyone opens Roamly, because it owns the moment the plan is formed.

In Module 1, I treated the market as a feature gap: users struggle with split payments, so we build them. The battlecard says the gap is real, but the competitor doesn’t win on features. It wins on timing and adoption cost. The group agrees on an activity in the chat, tracks RSVPs in a familiar app, and settles up with a tool everyone already has. By the time Roamly enters, the plan exists and the habit is set.

This also explains why the pain persists. Fronting money and chasing friends is spread across days of small annoyances, so it feels tolerable, and “tolerable” is why the free stack and “skip the experience entirely” are both stronger rivals than Viator or Airbnb.     
So the question isn’t “is our group product better?” It’s “can we get in at the moment the pain is felt, with less effort than doing nothing?”

Roamly Groups isn’t competing with other platforms. It’s competing with a free workflow that wins by showing up first, so it has to win by being the lowest-effort way to turn an agreed plan into a paid booking, with the organizer’s money risk removed.
